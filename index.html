Mission hardware guide

Explore the spacecraft, instruments, and engineering that made each mission work.

About this project

This student project highlights spacecraft, landers, telescopes, and experiments that remain in space or on other worlds. The idea is to show that exploration does not end when a mission is declared complete: some machines keep working for years, and even the ones that have gone quiet still have a lasting scientific legacy.

From the Moon to Mars, from deep space to Earth orbit, these objects carry on the work of discovery long after their launch. Some are still collecting data, while others continue to influence the next generation of missions by teaching us what is possible, what is fragile, and what still remains to be explored.

It is a small, human-scale guide to a much larger story: how NASA and partner missions leave behind traces of engineering, curiosity, and wonder that can still shape our understanding of the solar system decades later.

NASA hardware is still out there.

Since the 1960s, NASA machines have explored the Moon, Mars, and beyond. Some finished their missions long ago. Others are still helping us learn. Here are a few of their stories.

Moon 1969

Apollo 11's Moon mirror

A small experiment with a remarkably long afterlife.

Called a lunar laser ranging retroreflector, the array sends incoming light back toward its source. Because it has no moving parts or power supply, it can remain useful on the lunar surface for decades.

Chapter 1: A quiet installation

In July 21, 1969, astronauts on board Apollo 11 mission installed a small mirror device made up of corner-cube reflectors on the Moon’s surface. This was a device that would work even after the astronauts left the Moon.

Chapter 2: A signal across space

Observatories now direct a brief laser pulse towards the array. Some light particles travel from the observatory and back again, and the time taken is measured.

Chapter 3: Measuring a changing Moon

Multiple observations have helped in determining the distance between Earth and Moon with great accuracy. These enable scientists to examine the movement of the moon and the theory of gravity through it.

Status: Still useful — no batteries needed.
Mars 1976

Viking 1 lander

Viking 1 brought a full science laboratory to the Martian surface.

Chapter 1: Touchdown

Having traveled for a considerable distance from the Earth, the Viking 1 reached the planet Mars on 20 July 1976, marking the first successful landing of an American space probe on this alien world.

Chapter 2: A laboratory on another world

It took photographs of its rugged terrain, measured the climate conditions in its vicinity, and conducted tests on soil samples from Mars. It performed biological tests to detect the presence of microorganisms but could not prove their presence.

Chapter 3: The first long look

The visuals and data collected by Viking provided the first long-range look at the Martian landscape for scientists. Although the lander ultimately became dormant, its data proved invaluable in shaping future missions.

Status: Mission complete; the lander remains on Mars.
Mars 2012

Curiosity rover

A car-sized rover investigating whether ancient Mars could have supported microbial life.

Curiosity landed inside Gale Crater in August 2012. Its route across the crater floor and up Mount Sharp lets scientists examine layers of rock formed in different ancient environments.

Chapter 1: A landing inside an ancient crater

In August 2012, Curiosity landed on Mars in Gale Crater. It has started to investigate an area where the rocks contain information about the transformation of Mars over billions of years.

Chapter 2: Reading the rocks

With cameras, a drill, and instruments that study rock, soil, and air, Curiosity acts as a mobile field laboratory. It travels carefully, choosing places where each new layer can add another piece to the story.

Chapter 3: Following the water

It has discovered proof of the presence of an environment that contained liquid water and the right chemicals on early Mars. Curiosity is not in search of living beings on Mars but rather if the planet ever had the right conditions for life to thrive.

Chapter 4: The climb continues

While the rover roams around on Mount Sharp, it examines rocks of various periods in time. Every pause reveals to scientists a world that was much more diverse – and maybe even friendlier – than the harsh desert world we see now.

Status: Active — still exploring and sending home science.
Deep space 1977

Voyager 1

Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 turned a planetary tour into a journey beyond the Sun's bubble.

After its Saturn encounter, the spacecraft continued outward on a path that took it across the heliopause in 2012. Its great distance means radio messages take many hours to travel between Voyager and Earth.

Chapter 1: The outer planets

In 1977, Voyager 1 was launched to make up close encounters with Jupiter and Saturn. This spacecraft helped to discover active planets, complex rings, and moons, which were used for future investigations.

Chapter 2: Beyond the Sun's bubble

Saturn was followed by a further journey out into space. In 2012, the spacecraft crossed the heliopause and started sampling the space beyond the influence of the sun’s magnetic field.

Chapter 3: A distant conversation

However, it transmits information using radio waves from the Earth despite the great distance between it and the Earth where any transmission takes several hours. Some equipment continues sending information while the energy source gradually runs out.

Chapter 4: A greeting carried forward

Attached to the spacecraft is a golden record filled with sound, music, and greetings from our planet Earth. It acts as a time capsule for whoever finds it, while Voyager keeps traveling silently through the cosmos.

Status: Still traveling; some instruments continue to send data.
Earth orbit 1990

Hubble Space Telescope

Above Earth's atmosphere, Hubble captures sharp views across a wide range of light.

Chapter 1: Above the blur

The Hubble Telescope was launched into space in 1990. It began observing without the interference of Earth’s atmosphere that distorts incoming light. When there were some problems with its mirror, astronauts traveled to it and installed corrective lenses.

Chapter 2: A changing universe

Throughout the years, astronauts have returned to fix and improve Hubble. Hubble has been used to observe planets, star formation areas, and remote galaxies across different wavelengths..

Chapter 3: Looking back in time

As light needs time to move, distant observations reveal what our universe used to look like in the past. The photographs taken by Hubble allow scientists to study the formation and evolution of stars and galaxies.

Status: Active — still observing the universe from orbit.
Moon 2009

Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter

LRO is building a detailed picture of the Moon to support science and future exploration.

Chapter 1: A new map of the Moon

The Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter was put into lunar orbit in 2009. Using their instruments from the sky above, they were able to map and take measurements that showed how diverse the surface of the moon can be.

Chapter 2: Searching the shadows

Some of the craters in the polar areas never get direct sunlight and are very cold. The studies by LRO help the scientists study such areas.

Chapter 3: Preparing to return

These tools enable researchers to analyze various possible landing sites and to plan for future exploration. Every time that we orbit, it brings more information about the regions where we will one day operate on the Moon again.

Status: Active — still orbiting and mapping the Moon.
Deep space 1972

Pioneer 10

Pioneer 10 opened the way for later missions to the outer Solar System.

Chapter 1: A first crossing

The spacecraft Pioneer 10 was launched in 1972 on a journey towards the distant parts of the Solar System. The year 1973 brought it a record-breaking encounter with the planet Jupiter, which showed that it is possible for a craft to travel through the asteroid belt.

Chapter 2: Meeting Jupiter

Pioneer took pictures and made measurements of the planet and its environment as it flew by the planet. This helped scientists understand what the environment around Jupiter was like and paved the way for future spacecraft.

Chapter 3: A signal fades

Pioneer 10 kept going after the encounter mission. The spacecraft sent its last signal in 2003, and it is now silent, but continues on its journey through space.

Status: Silent but still drifting outward in deep space.
Mars 2021

Perseverance rover

The rover searching for ancient biosignatures and collecting samples for the future.

Chapter 1: Jezero Crater

The rover Perseverance touched down on Mars in 2021 within Jezero Crater, which once hosted a lake containing minerals that could hold evidence of habitable conditions.

Chapter 2: A powered field lab

The cameras, drills, and scientific tools will be used to examine rocks and dust on the planet and test equipment that can be used on future missions to Mars.

Chapter 3: Sample return prep

Perseverance is storing up samples that one day may be returned back to Earth, thus building a connection between today’s exploration and future analysis on Earth.

Status: Active — still exploring and collecting scientifically valuable samples.
Saturn 1997

Cassini

One of the most revealing investigations of the ringed planet and its moons.

Chapter 1: Arrival

Cassini arrived at Saturn in 2004 after an extensive interplanetary voyage, embarking on a 13-year mission that changed our perspective about the planet’s system.

Chapter 2: Ring and moon science

The spacecraft mapped out the complex ring system of the planet, analyzed the atmosphere of Saturn and discovered active worlds like Enceladus and Titan.

Chapter 3: End of mission

Cassini deliberately terminated its mission with a plunge into the Saturnian atmosphere in 2017 to safeguard satellites such as Enceladus.

Status: Mission complete; its legacy continues in the data it returned.
Moon 2019

Chang'e 4

The first mission to make a soft landing on the far side of the Moon.

Chapter 1: A hidden hemisphere

Landing Site: The Chinese probe Chang’e 4 made its landing on the surface of the Von Kármán crater on the Moon’s far side for the first time ever.

Chapter 2: Lunar communication

It utilized a relay satellite that was placed in space to communicate with the far side, which is inaccessible directly from Earth.

Chapter 3: A scientific first

The mission investigated the lunar surface, analyzed its surrounding environment, and captured images that have revolutionized our scientific perception of the Moon’s far side.

Status: Active — still operating and relaying lunar data.
Venus 1989

Magellan

Venus’s cloud-shrouded surface was mapped in extraordinary detail by radar.

Chapter 1: Radar from orbit

Magellan employed the use of radar technology in order to be able to see through the dense clouds surrounding Venus and create a very accurate map of the planet's surface.

Chapter 2: A world of volcanoes

The maps provided an indication of vast regions of volcanoes and geologically formed landscapes. This information widened the knowledge about the sister planet of the earth.

Chapter 3: A long afterlife

After many years of functioning, Magellan concluded its project by entering the atmosphere of Venus in 1994. The results obtained have remained basic until nowadays.

Status: Mission complete; its radar maps are still used by scientists.
Moon 1971

Apollo 15 lunar rover

A fold-out mobility system that made the lunar surface feel much larger.

Chapter 1: The first long-distance drive

Dave Scott and Jim Irwin rode in the Lunar Roving Vehicle to distances away from the point of touchdown on the lunar surface, exploring much more territory than previous moonwalkers on foot were able to do.

Chapter 2: Science on wheels

The rover was loaded with equipment, samples, and supplies for the astronauts while allowing them to collect rock samples and examine the lunar surface over a large area. It extended the brief stop into an extensive geological study.

Chapter 3: A lasting footprint

Although the mission had been completed, the marks and hardware left behind by the rover were left as a record of the expanded range that astronauts could achieve. It illustrated the idea that having mobility could alter what science the crew could conduct away from its landing site.

Status: Mission complete; the rover remains on the Moon.
Mars 2004

Opportunity rover

A small robot that outlived its expected mission by years and changed how we think about Mars.

Chapter 1: A long journey to an ancient plain

Opportunity landed in the Meridiani Planum region in 2004, which had signs of having a past filled with water. It was only supposed to last for 90 sols, but it exhibited a surprisingly resilient spirit.

Chapter 2: The science kept moving

It roamed across the surface of Mars, analyzed rocks and soil, and discovered evidence of liquid water having existed on the Red Planet. The findings allowed linking the conditions on Mars long ago to the emergence of life on Earth.

Chapter 3: A silent end

After a planetwide dust storm occurred in 2018, contact was lost with Opportunity. The rover came to symbolize perseverance and expanded the realm of science further than anyone could have imagined.

Status: Mission complete; the rover remains silent on Mars.
Deep space 2006

New Horizons

A compact mission that transformed a distant world from a blurry point into a real place.

Chapter 1: A route to Pluto

New Horizons was launched in 2006 on a quest to deliver scientific insights about the dwarf planet and its moons from a relatively close range. The spacecraft got there in 2015 after almost a decade-long journey.

Chapter 2: Looking past the familiar

Its images uncovered the presence of mountains, glaciers, plains, and weather on Pluto. The mission proved that a planet thought to be cold and dormant could indeed be geologically alive and diverse.

Chapter 3: A path into the Kuiper Belt

However, after visiting Pluto, New Horizons continued on its journey further out into the Solar System, exploring icy, small worlds in a region that was virtually impossible to explore in such a way before.

Status: Active — still flying farther into the outer Solar System.
Jupiter 2011

Juno

An orbiting laboratory working close to Jupiter to reveal the giant planet's inner structure.

Chapter 1: A dangerous orbit

Juno arrived at Jupiter in 2016 and started making passes through Jupiter’s clouds, gathering information from an area that is so difficult and extreme that other spacecraft could not survive there.

Chapter 2: Measuring the giant

Its tools were used to measure Jupiter's magnetic and gravitational fields, study its atmosphere, and gain insights into its formation process and atmospheric dynamics.

Chapter 3: A persistent observer

The spacecraft is still active today, sending information about a planet which seems to be both familiar and mysterious at the same time. This reminds us that even such huge planets are capable of surprising a continuing mission.

Status: Active — still orbiting Jupiter and returning science.

Why leave machines behind?

Even when people are back at home, the spacecraft will continue to measure, photograph, and provide hints. Even an unmanned device will be capable of leaving behind a worthwhile experiment or a significant landmark.
