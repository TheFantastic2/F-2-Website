Mission hardware guide

Explore the spacecraft, instruments, and engineering that made each mission work.

About this project

This student project highlights spacecraft, landers, telescopes, and experiments that remain in space or on other worlds. The idea is to show that exploration does not end when a mission is declared complete: some machines keep working for years, and even the ones that have gone quiet still have a lasting scientific legacy.

From the Moon to Mars, from deep space to Earth orbit, these objects carry on the work of discovery long after their launch. Some are still collecting data, while others continue to influence the next generation of missions by teaching us what is possible, what is fragile, and what still remains to be explored.

It is a small, human-scale guide to a much larger story: how NASA and partner missions leave behind traces of engineering, curiosity, and wonder that can still shape our understanding of the solar system decades later.

NASA hardware is still out there.

Since the 1960s, NASA machines have explored the Moon, Mars, and beyond. Some finished their missions long ago. Others are still helping us learn. Here are a few of their stories.

Moon 1969

Apollo 11's Moon mirror

A small experiment with a remarkably long afterlife.

Called a lunar laser ranging retroreflector, the array sends incoming light back toward its source. Because it has no moving parts or power supply, it can remain useful on the lunar surface for decades.

Chapter 1: A quiet installation

During Apollo 11’s final moonwalk, Buzz Aldrin placed a small panel on the lunar ground. Its 100 corner-cube prisms were designed to send laser light back toward its source. The astronauts would soon leave, but this instrument had no return trip to make: it was built to stay where it was and keep working.

Chapter 2: A signal across space

From observatories on Earth, scientists aim short laser pulses at the panel. The beam spreads across the distance, and almost all of its light misses. A few photons make the round trip. By timing their return, researchers measure the Earth–Moon distance with remarkable precision, then repeat the experiment to see how that distance changes.

Chapter 3: Measuring a changing Moon

The reflector has no batteries, radio, or moving parts; its prism shapes do the work. Along with other lunar reflectors, it has helped scientists track the Moon’s orbit and test ideas about gravity. A device carried to the surface for a short experiment became part of a record spanning decades—a quiet exchange of light between two worlds.

Status: Still useful — no batteries needed.
Mars 1976

Viking 1 lander

Viking 1 brought a full science laboratory to the Martian surface.

Chapter 1: Touchdown

After a ten-month journey, Viking 1 separated from its orbiter and descended toward Chryse Planitia. On July 20, 1976, it became the first U.S. spacecraft to land successfully on Mars. Its first pictures showed rocks scattered across a dusty plain beneath a hazy sky. For scientists on Earth, that ordinary-looking horizon was extraordinary: Mars had become a place they could study from the ground.

Chapter 2: A laboratory on another world

Viking’s cameras watched the landscape while weather sensors measured wind, pressure, and temperature. Its robotic arm reached out, scooped soil, and delivered samples to instruments inside the lander. Three biology experiments searched for signs of living organisms. The results were intriguing, but they did not prove that life was present. Scientists kept debating what the tests meant, and later Mars missions carried new ways to investigate the question.

Chapter 3: The first long look

Viking 1 returned pictures and measurements for years, giving later landing teams a real place to compare with views from orbit. It watched the weather change and repeatedly studied the same patch of ground. When contact finally ended in 1982, the lander stayed on the plain. Its radio went quiet, but its maps, experiments, and lessons continued to shape how engineers planned the next visits to Mars.

Status: Mission complete; the lander remains on Mars.
Mars 2012

Curiosity rover

A car-sized rover investigating whether ancient Mars could have supported microbial life.

Curiosity landed inside Gale Crater in August 2012. Its route across the crater floor and up Mount Sharp lets scientists examine layers of rock formed in different ancient environments.

Chapter 1: A landing inside an ancient crater

In August 2012, Curiosity landed in Gale Crater using a rocket-powered sky crane. Cables lowered the rover onto the ground; once its wheels touched, the crane cut loose and flew away. Ahead stood Mount Sharp, a stack of rock layers built over a long span of Martian history. Curiosity’s route up the mountain would let scientists read those layers one stop at a time.

Chapter 2: Reading the rocks

Curiosity moves slowly, stopping when a rock deserves a closer look. Its cameras photograph a target; a laser can zap a tiny spot and analyze the flash, while a drill can collect powder from inside the stone. Instruments in the rover study the sample. Mission scientists plan each drive from Earth, then wait for the images and measurements to cross space before choosing what to do next.

Chapter 3: Following the water

The rocks show that Gale Crater once held lakes and streams. Curiosity has found chemicals and environments that could have supported microbial life billions of years ago, but it has not found proof that life existed. The rover is not searching for living organisms today. It is piecing together the old conditions: what the water was like, how long it lasted, and how the landscape changed when it disappeared.

Chapter 4: The climb continues

Curiosity is still climbing Mount Sharp and sending home its findings. A wheel track marks its route; a drilled hole lets the team compare one layer of rock with another. The mountain is too large to understand from a single view, so the rover works like a field geologist with a very long commute. Every careful stop adds another part to the story of a Mars that was once wetter and more varied.

Status: Active — still exploring and sending home science.
Deep space 1977

Voyager 1

Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 turned a planetary tour into a journey beyond the Sun's bubble.

After its Saturn encounter, the spacecraft continued outward on a path that took it across the heliopause in 2012. Its great distance means radio messages take many hours to travel between Voyager and Earth.

Chapter 1: The outer planets

Voyager 1 launched in 1977 and passed Jupiter in 1979, then Saturn in 1980. Its cameras sent home close views of striped clouds, rings, and moons that had been no more than faint dots from Earth. At Jupiter, it saw volcanoes erupting on Io. The Saturn encounter bent its path northward, away from the planets. The flybys were brief; the discoveries gave scientists years of new questions to work on.

Chapter 2: Beyond the Sun's bubble

In 1990, Voyager 1 turned its camera back toward home and photographed Earth as a tiny point in a sunbeam. The image became known as the Pale Blue Dot. In 2012, the spacecraft crossed the heliopause, where the solar wind gives way to interstellar space. There was no visible border; its instruments detected the change in particles and magnetic conditions around it.

Chapter 3: A distant conversation

The radio signal now takes nearly a day to travel one way between Voyager 1 and Earth. Engineers send a command and wait hours for it to arrive; a reply takes hours more. The spacecraft’s power supply is slowly fading, so the team switches off equipment in a planned order. A few instruments still send readings about the space around the probe, a thin conversation from farther away than any other spacecraft has traveled.

Chapter 4: A greeting carried forward

A Golden Record rides aboard Voyager 1, carrying greetings, music, and sounds chosen to represent life on Earth. The spacecraft is not headed toward a known destination; it continues along its path through the galaxy. The record may never be found, but it carries a small portrait of the world that launched it. Voyager’s cameras have gone dark, yet its remaining instruments continue to report what lies beyond the Sun’s reach.

Status: Still traveling; some instruments continue to send data.
Earth orbit 1990

Hubble Space Telescope

Above Earth's atmosphere, Hubble captures sharp views across a wide range of light.

Chapter 1: Above the blur

Hubble launched aboard the space shuttle Discovery in 1990. Above Earth’s atmosphere, it could observe light that the air absorbs or blurs. But the first images showed stars with fuzzy halos instead of sharp points. A tiny flaw in the shape of the main mirror had thrown the focus off. The telescope was working in orbit, but its view was not yet the one scientists had planned for.

Chapter 2: A changing universe

In 1993, astronauts flew to Hubble and installed corrective optics and a new camera. The next images showed that the repair worked. Shuttle crews returned several more times, replacing instruments, gyroscopes, and other equipment. Each visit took careful work in bulky suits around a telescope moving above Earth at thousands of kilometers per hour. Hubble became an observatory that people could repair and improve while it was already in space.

Chapter 3: Looking back in time

Hubble has photographed planets, star-forming clouds, and galaxies whose light began its journey billions of years ago. It does not look into the past by magic; the light simply takes time to reach us. The telescope is still working today. Its story includes brilliant images, but also a practical lesson: a problem was found, a repair was designed, and a mission that began with a blurry view went on to change astronomy.

Status: Active — still observing the universe from orbit.
Moon 2009

Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter

LRO is building a detailed picture of the Moon to support science and future exploration.

Chapter 1: A new map of the Moon

The Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter arrived at the Moon in 2009 and began circling it with cameras and measuring instruments. It photographs the ground in strips, returning to the same places from different angles. Over time, those passes form a detailed map. LRO has photographed Apollo landing sites and rugged regions that crews have never visited, turning a changing view from orbit into a record scientists can compare.

Chapter 2: Searching the shadows

Near the lunar poles, some deep craters stay in shadow for long stretches and become extremely cold. LRO measures their temperatures and searches for signs of water ice and other materials. A camera cannot see everything in a dark crater, so scientists combine pictures with measurements from other instruments. The shadows are difficult places to study, but they may hold clues about what is available on the Moon and what future explorers would face.

Chapter 3: Preparing to return

LRO also measures elevation and tracks how sunlight falls across the surface. A place that looks smooth in a photograph may hide a steep slope, a boulder field, or a long shadow. Those details help planners compare landing sites and prepare for future exploration. The orbiter is still making its rounds, adding observations with each pass. Its maps help scientists study the Moon now and help future crews choose where to go.

Status: Active — still orbiting and mapping the Moon.
Deep space 1972

Pioneer 10

Pioneer 10 opened the way for later missions to the outer Solar System.

Chapter 1: A first crossing

Pioneer 10 left Earth in March 1972, headed for Jupiter and a region no spacecraft had crossed before. Engineers worried that dust or small rocks in the asteroid belt might damage the probe, but it passed through safely. In December 1973, Pioneer reached Jupiter. Before it arrived, no one knew exactly how harsh the planet’s radiation belts would be for a spacecraft flying nearby.

Chapter 2: Meeting Jupiter

Pioneer swept past the giant planet, taking the first close images while its instruments measured magnetic fields, radiation, and particles. The brief encounter gave scientists a much better picture of Jupiter and helped engineers prepare later spacecraft for the environment they would face. Then the planet’s gravity bent Pioneer’s path outward. It had finished its main job, but the probe did not stop at Jupiter or turn back toward Earth.

Chapter 3: A signal fades

A small aluminum plaque attached to Pioneer 10 carries a drawing of a human pair and a map pointing toward Earth’s place among nearby stars. The probe continued sending a faint signal until 2003, when Earth received its last contact. Pioneer is silent now, but still moving outward. Its story has two parts: the radio conversation ended, while the spacecraft itself continued along the route it helped open.

Status: Silent but still drifting outward in deep space.
Deep space 1977

Voyager 2

Continuing its grand tour of the outer planets, sending back data from the edge of the heliosphere.

Voyager 2 followed a path that took it past Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, giving humanity its first close-up views of the giant outer worlds and the space between them.

Chapter 1: A grand tour

Voyager 2 launched in August 1977, before Voyager 1, and followed a route shaped by the gravity of the giant planets. It passed Jupiter and Saturn, then continued toward Uranus and Neptune. Each flyby lasted only a short time, so the team planned which pictures and measurements mattered most before the spacecraft arrived. Voyager 2 gave people their first close views of worlds that had been distant points of light.

Chapter 2: Ice giants revealed

At Uranus in 1986 and Neptune in 1989, Voyager 2 found rings, moons, and changing weather. It remains the only spacecraft to have visited either planet. The pictures showed that these ice giants were not quiet blue balls: they had complex atmospheres and systems of their own. The flybys were brief, but scientists are still using the measurements to ask questions about how the planets formed and why they look so different.

Chapter 3: Still out there

After Neptune, Voyager 2 continued outward and crossed the heliopause into interstellar space in 2018. Some instruments still send measurements from far beyond the planets. Engineers conserve its shrinking power supply by turning off equipment in a planned order, while radio antennas on Earth listen for the faint signal. A Golden Record rides aboard, carrying sounds and images from Earth as the spacecraft travels on with no known destination.

Status: Active — still transmitting data from the outer solar system.
Mars 2021

Perseverance rover

The rover searching for ancient biosignatures and collecting samples for the future.

Chapter 1: Jezero Crater

Perseverance touched down in Jezero Crater in February 2021. From orbit, scientists had spotted a fan-shaped delta where a river may once have emptied into a lake. On the ground, the rover could study the layers directly. Places like this can preserve clues about ancient environments, so Perseverance is looking for signs that Mars may once have been habitable—not for living organisms today.

Chapter 2: A powered field lab

Perseverance works like a mobile field lab. Cameras help the team choose a rock; a drill on the robotic arm cuts a small core from inside it. The rover seals samples in clean tubes and records exactly where each one came from. It also tests equipment for future exploration. Every drive, photograph, and sample location adds context, so the rock can be understood as part of a landscape rather than an isolated object.

Chapter 3: Sample return prep

Some sample tubes have been placed on the Martian ground as a backup cache, while others remain stored inside the rover. They are being kept for possible collection by a future mission; exactly how they might reach Earth is still being worked out. If returned, the samples could be studied in laboratories with tools too large to send to Mars. For now, Perseverance is gathering the evidence and keeping careful track of its route.

Status: Active — still exploring and collecting scientifically valuable samples.
Saturn 1997

Cassini

One of the most revealing investigations of the ringed planet and its moons.

Chapter 1: Arrival

Cassini reached Saturn in 2004 after a seven-year trip, becoming the first spacecraft to orbit the planet. It carried Europe’s Huygens probe, which separated from Cassini and descended through Titan’s thick haze in 2005. Huygens sent pictures and measurements from the surface before its batteries ran down. For the first time, scientists could study the ground of a moon in the outer Solar System from a probe that had landed there.

Chapter 2: Ring and moon science

Cassini circled Saturn for thirteen years, passing the rings and returning to the same moons from new angles. At Enceladus, it flew through water-rich plumes spraying from cracks near the south pole. At Titan, radar revealed lakes and seas of liquid methane and ethane beneath the haze. These moons became destinations in their own right, with weather, geology, and new questions about what makes a world active.

Chapter 3: End of mission

By 2017, Cassini was running low on fuel. Mission controllers sent it through a final series of dives between Saturn and its rings, then guided it into the planet’s atmosphere. The planned end helped protect moons such as Enceladus from accidental contamination. Cassini stopped transmitting, but the archive it left behind is still being studied. Its last plunge was a careful choice by a team trying to protect the very places the spacecraft had made more interesting.

Status: Mission complete; its legacy continues in the data it returned.
Venus 1989

Magellan

Venus’s cloud-shrouded surface was mapped in extraordinary detail by radar.

Chapter 1: Radar from orbit

When Magellan reached Venus in 1990, thick clouds hid the ground from ordinary cameras. The spacecraft used radar instead: it sent radio pulses toward the surface and measured the echoes that came back. Each pass added another strip to the map. By combining them, scientists could see the shape and texture of the planet beneath the clouds for the first time in such detail.

Chapter 2: A world of volcanoes

The map showed broad plains, ridges, impact craters, and many volcanic features across nearly the whole planet. Venus was no longer a bright disk hidden behind clouds; it had a landscape scientists could compare and study. Radar could not answer every question about how the features formed, but it revealed where to look and gave researchers a way to understand a world whose surface sunlight could not show.

Chapter 3: A long afterlife

After years of mapping, controllers lowered Magellan’s orbit until it entered Venus’s atmosphere in October 1994. The spacecraft broke apart, but its radar maps remained. Researchers still compare those records with later observations to study the planet. Magellan’s mission ended in the clouds it had spent years looking through; its most lasting contribution was the detailed picture it sent home before it disappeared.

Status: Mission complete; its radar maps are still used by scientists.
Moon 1971

Apollo 15 lunar rover

A fold-out mobility system that made the lunar surface feel much larger.

Chapter 1: The first long-distance drive

The Lunar Roving Vehicle traveled to the Moon folded against Apollo 15’s Lunar Module. Once outside, David Scott and James Irwin unfolded the frame, checked the wheels and controls, and climbed into their seats. The small electric rover let them travel much farther from the landing site than a spacesuit walk could manage. A ridge that had looked distant from the lander was suddenly reachable—and the crew could still bring its samples back.

Chapter 2: Science on wheels

Four wire-mesh wheels, each with its own electric motor, carried the astronauts across the uneven ground. A hand controller steered the rover, and a navigation display helped them track direction and distance. Scott and Irwin drove to selected stops, photographed the terrain, and collected rocks. The rover did not choose what mattered; it gave the astronauts more room to look, compare, and decide where the next piece of the Moon’s story might be.

Chapter 3: A lasting footprint

Across three excursions, Apollo 15’s crew traveled nearly 28 kilometers. Apollo 16 and Apollo 17 carried the other two lunar rovers. At the end of each mission, the crews left the vehicles behind and climbed back into their Lunar Modules. Their tracks remain beside them, marking the routes people drove on another world. The rover extended the astronauts’ reach, then became part of the landscape it had helped them explore.

Status: Mission complete; the rover remains on the Moon.
Mars 2004

Opportunity rover

A small robot that outlived its expected mission by years and changed how we think about Mars.

Chapter 1: A long journey to an ancient plain

Opportunity landed on Meridiani Planum in January 2004. Its planned mission was about 90 Martian days, or sols. Soon after landing, the rover found rocks with minerals and textures that form in the presence of water. A small robot built for a short survey had found a reason to keep moving. The team began choosing the next nearby target, then the next, turning a brief visit into a long field investigation.

Chapter 2: The science kept moving

Opportunity drove from one outcrop to another, taking pictures before brushing or grinding a thin patch from a rock. Instruments examined what lay beneath the dusty surface. The rover found strong evidence that liquid water had once altered Martian rocks. It could not tell scientists whether life had existed, but it helped show that some ancient places on Mars had once been wet. Each stop added another point to a route more than 45 kilometers long.

Chapter 3: A silent end

Opportunity worked for more than fourteen years—far beyond its planned 90 sols. In 2018, a planet-wide dust storm blocked sunlight from its solar panels and the rover stopped responding. NASA sent commands for months, but no reply came; the mission was declared complete in 2019. Opportunity remains at its last location. Its story ends with a quiet radio, but the images and measurements it sent home are still part of how scientists understand Mars.

Status: Mission complete; the rover remains silent on Mars.
Deep space 2006

New Horizons

A compact mission that transformed a distant world from a blurry point into a real place.

Chapter 1: A route to Pluto

New Horizons launched in 2006 and spent years crossing the outer Solar System. In July 2015, it swept past Pluto at high speed. It could not slow down to orbit, so every observation had to fit into a short window as the spacecraft rushed by. Cameras and instruments turned toward Pluto and its moons, gathering images and readings that would take hours to travel back across the radio link to Earth.

Chapter 2: Looking past the familiar

The pictures turned Pluto from a blurry point into a landscape of mountains made of water ice, broad frozen plains, and a thin atmosphere. Some areas looked surprisingly smooth and young, hinting that Pluto’s surface had changed more recently than expected. The team received the data slowly, piece by piece, then assembled them into maps. A distant world that had seemed small and simple became a place with its own weather and geology.

Chapter 3: A path into the Kuiper Belt

After Pluto, New Horizons continued into the Kuiper Belt and flew past Arrokoth in 2019. The small object looked like two rounded pieces joined together, preserving clues about how early building blocks of the Solar System came together. New Horizons kept traveling after the encounter, studying its surroundings. Pluto had been the reason for the mission, but the spacecraft’s path carried the exploration farther than its original destination.

Status: Active — still flying farther into the outer Solar System.
Jupiter 2011

Juno

An orbiting laboratory working close to Jupiter to reveal the giant planet's inner structure.

Chapter 1: A dangerous orbit

Juno reached Jupiter in 2016 after a five-year journey. It follows a long, looping orbit over the planet’s poles, briefly passing close to the cloud tops before swinging far away again. The route limits how long the spacecraft spends inside Jupiter’s intense radiation belts. Far from the Sun, its large solar panels collect the light needed to power the instruments—an engineering choice that makes the mission’s location possible.

Chapter 2: Measuring the giant

On each close pass, Juno measures Jupiter’s gravity and magnetic field and studies signals changed by the atmosphere. Those readings help scientists estimate how deep the famous cloud bands reach and what the planet may look like below them. The camera sends home views of storms and cloud patterns, while other instruments gather less visible measurements. The team pieces the results together pass by pass, like building a map from brief visits.

Chapter 3: A persistent observer

Juno has also flown close to large moons such as Ganymede, Europa, and Io. Each encounter turns a small point beside Jupiter into a world with its own surface and questions. The spacecraft continues returning science from its orbit. Its pictures bring the giant planet’s storms close, while its measurements help researchers investigate what lies under the clouds and how Jupiter and its moons formed.

Status: Active — still orbiting Jupiter and returning science.

Why leave machines behind?

Even when people are back at home, the spacecraft will continue to measure, photograph, and provide hints. Even an unmanned device will be capable of leaving behind a worthwhile experiment or a significant landmark.
